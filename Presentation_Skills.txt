Reverse Outcome

How to Apply Reverse
Outcome Design
Step 1: Define the Final Clarity
Ask:
“If they remember only one thing, what should it be?”
Example (Function):
“They should know that functions exist to avoid
repetition.”
This is your anchor. Everything must serve this
idea.

Reverse Outcome
Design Drill
How to Apply Reverse

Step 2: Identify the Key Confusion to Prevent
Ask:
“What likely misunderstanding could weaken this
clarity?”
Example (Function):
“They might think functions must always take
parameters.”
Now your structure has direction:
You are intentionally guiding them from confusion to clarity.

-- 18 of 58 --

3-Layer
Planning Model
The Order That Protects
Understanding
Every topic must be built in three clean
cognitive layers.
Context
Core Idea(Set the definition)
Details
If you reverse this order, confusion appears.

-- 19 of 58 --

3-Layer
Planning Model
Layer 1 — Context
Before defining anything, create a reason to listen.
Why does this topic matter?
Example: Instead of starting with definition, start
with: “Yesterday we printed the same message five
times. If we want to change it, how many places do we
edit?”
Students: “Five.”
Tutor: “Do you think that this is efficient?”
Now the brain has a problem.
And the word “function” will feel like a solution.

-- 20 of 58 --

3-Layer
Planning Model
Layer 2 — Core Idea
After the problem is clear, set a clear definition for the
main topic without any further details:
What is this concept really doing?
Example:
“So instead of rewriting code, we create one reusable
action.That reusable action is called a function.”
Layer 2 must be clean and focused.
Now students understand what a function
fundamentally is.

-- 21 of 58 --

3-Layer
Planning Model
Layer 3 — Details
In this layer you answer the question of How
this topic is actually work ?
Discuss any further related topics in details,
show code, more examples,..
Example:
“This reusable action sometimes needs input to
work differently.That input is called a parameter.”
This supports the idea.

Visual & Nonverbal
Presence
Before you talk
Your physical presence shapes perception, trust, and
authority before your content even begins.
Notes:
First impressions form within seconds.
Nonverbal cues often carry more influence than words in
initial evaluation.
Confidence perception is driven strongly by posture,
facial control, and movement stability.
This component answers one question:
👉 What does the audience feel about you before they
process what you say?

Visual & Nonverbal
Presence
Grounding (Stability First)
If your body looks unstable, your message feels
unstable.
Grounding means:
Straight posture
Shoulders relaxed
Balanced stance or seated position
Controlled breathing
Starting only after you are fully still
Rule:
Before speaking → Pause → Breathe → Then start.
That pause builds authority.

-- 27 of 58 --

Visual & Nonverbal
Presence
Eye Direction
(Connection Control)
Eyes signal leadership.
If you avoid them, you lose authority.
Strong Presence:
When stating something important → direct eye
engagement.
When explaining details → natural scanning.
When transitioning → intentional shift.

-- 28 of 58 --

Visual & Nonverbal
Presence
Facial Alignment
(Emotion Match)
Your face must match your message.
If you explain something exciting with a flat
face → energy drops.
If you smile during serious explanation →
confusion increases.
Alignment builds trust and support your ideas .

-- 29 of 58 --

Visual & Nonverbal
Presence
Movement Control
Uncontrolled movement = distracting environment.
Strong movement looks like:
Gesture when explaining size, contrast, or structure.
Stillness when making key point.
Walking only when transitioning ideas.
Stillness increases authority, Movement increases
energy.Balance both.
